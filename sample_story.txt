The Last Light of Cape Morrow

Old Elias had kept the lighthouse at Cape Morrow for forty years. Every evening, as the sun dipped below the horizon, he climbed the worn stone steps to light the great lamp. His weathered hands knew every groove in the iron railing, every chip in the glass lens.

One autumn night, a fierce storm rolled in from the Atlantic. The wind howled like a wounded animal, and waves crashed against the rocks with enough force to shake the tower. Elias watched from the lamp room as a small fishing boat appeared through the rain, struggling against the swells. He recognized the boat — it belonged to young Mara, the fisherman's daughter from the village below.

Elias cranked the lamp to its brightest setting and angled the beam toward the treacherous rocks near the harbor entrance. The light cut through the sheets of rain like a golden sword. Down in the boat, Mara spotted the beam and adjusted her course, steering away from the jagged rocks that had claimed so many vessels before.

The storm raged for hours. Elias never left his post, feeding oil to the lamp, keeping the light steady and strong. When dawn finally broke, pale and exhausted, the sea had calmed. Mara's boat sat safely in the harbor, battered but whole.

That morning, Mara climbed the hill to the lighthouse. She found Elias sitting in his chair by the lamp, a tired smile on his face. Without a word, she set a basket of fresh fish at his feet — the same quiet thanks her father had given, and his father before him. Some lights, they both knew, were worth keeping.
